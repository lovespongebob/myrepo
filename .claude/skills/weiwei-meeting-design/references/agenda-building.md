## The design process applied to meetings

A meeting is well defined as: *something that enables an outcome we can't
otherwise achieve, measured in an agreed-upon fashion.* Anything else — "the
thing we do on Tuesdays" — is a habit, not a tool.

Apply the four-step design-thinking loop (after Tim Brown) to any meeting,
especially recurring ones:

1. **Identify the problem** the meeting is intended to solve; research it
   before committing to a meeting. Vague goals ("keep things from getting out
   of hand") produce vague meetings — push for measurable job-performance
   indicators (fewer process steps, more ideas produced, a decision made).
2. **Consider multiple formats.** Conversation-only is a single, often faulty
   pattern. Alternatives: visualizing a process on a wall, silent writing
   before discussion, manipulable objects (sticky notes), time-limited
   speaking turns, breaking into subgroups. Consider skipping a recurring
   meeting once, just to see what happens.
3. **Make small changes and assess.** Introduce one change (e.g., a countdown
   timer per speaker, a public recorder), observe against the baseline, keep
   what works. A meeting stuck on one unexamined format is flying on autopilot:
   same voices dominating, people tuning out, original intent forgotten.
4. **Walk away when the job is done.** For every standing meeting ask: Why was
   this established? Has that job been done? If the first has no answer or the
   second is "yes," delete or decline it.

Cheaper tools than meetings for pure information flow: chat channels with a
stated purpose, email, shared documents. Reserve synchronous time for what
needs synchrony: building shared understanding, resolving conflict, making
decisions together.

## Memory: the universal design constraint

Every attendee brings the same constraint: a brain.

- **Working memory** holds roughly the last 30 seconds of what was seen and
  heard, and capacity varies between people. Just because you remember what
  was said two minutes ago doesn't mean everyone does. Design response: set a
  pace slower than your own train of thought, in evenly sized chunks.
- **Intermediate-term memory** (2–3 hours) is where meeting content becomes
  post-meeting action. Sessions that run past ~90 minutes without a break
  degrade it — action items blur and get liberally reinterpreted. Design
  response: break long sessions at or before 90 minutes; turn key points into
  artifacts (diagrams, photos of the wall) that survive the meeting.
- **Sight and sound are processed separately** (Baddeley & Hitch's working
  memory model). Complementary visuals + speech improve retention;
  *competing* visuals (slides of the same words being spoken aloud) reduce it.
  Show a diagram or photo while talking, never a wall of bullet text read
  aloud.
- **Listening alone decays fast**: after 20–30 minutes of passive listening,
  absorption drops sharply (Bligh's lecture research). Design response: cap
  any continuous listening block at 20–30 minutes and follow each with
  reflection — discussion, Q&A, or an applied exercise.
- **Aggressive time limits help**: too much time in one thought-space invites
  the inner critic and rambling. Short, timed bursts get obvious ideas out
  fast and push toward the interesting ones.
- **Food matters in long workshops**: simple carbs (pastries, soda) spike and
  crash energy; protein and fat (eggs, yogurt, nuts, whole grains) sustain it.
  Plan catering for workshops over half a day.

## Input modes: listening, seeing, touching

Design for at least two modes in any substantive meeting:

- **Visual listening / public recording**: replace the private note-taker with
  a recorder who captures only the main ideas, conflicts, and decisions —
  large, visible to everyone, in real time. This creates a feedback loop
  (people correct mistakes on the spot), lets attendees switch between
  listening and looking, and produces "group memory" that outlives the room.
  A photo of the wall distributed afterward beats pages of prose notes nobody
  rereads.
- **Manipulatives**: sticky notes, cards, and rough physical prototypes engage
  spatial reasoning. Arranging ideas in space (grouping, sequencing, voting
  with dots) builds stronger shared memory than talking about the same ideas.
- **Writing before speaking**: a target length (one sentence) makes people
  consider what they say, equalizes loud and quiet voices, and speeds the
  meeting up.

## The three agenda elements and the math

Every agenda is built from three countable things: **ideas**, **people**, and
**time**. Being crystal clear about these three — while staying flexible about
everything else — keeps an agenda from being brittle. A well-designed agenda
still works when it's "mostly broken" by interruptions.

### Ideas

A **just-complex-enough concept** is a single fact or phenomenon expressible in
a sentence or two — what fits on a sticky note. Count how many the meeting must
cover; that number sets the meeting's scale.

Miller's "magical number seven plus or minus two": people hold about 5–7
complex concepts over about 10 minutes. Use **groups of five**:

- Present 5 concepts in ~10 minutes.
- Stop and review/discuss/apply for another ~10 minutes.
- That 20-minute unit is one **content section**.

So one hour ≈ 3 content sections ≈ 15 concepts — and planning slightly under
that leaves room for productive tangents. "We've got a lot to get through, so
let's dive right in" followed by 15 rapid-fire points is a design failure, not
a time-saver: earlier points simply evaporate.

If you aren't running the meeting but the pace is out of control, count
concepts and ask to pause and review around the fifth — it reinforces ideas
for everyone and nudges the presenter into better structure.

### People

Shared understanding requires pairwise agreement. **Points of agreement between
n people = n(n-1)/2**: 3 people → 3; 5 → 10; 7 → 21; 12 → 66. Complexity
scales quadratically with each added attendee.

- Cap a single conversation at **6–7 people**. Invite only people vital to the
  outcome.
- If more must attend, write down why each person is there and tell them
  (in the invite, or restated at the start), then **split into subgroups of
  5–7** (8 people → two groups of four; 15 → three groups of five). Each
  subgroup converges on one output, then groups share out and reconcile —
  which collapses 66 points of agreement into a handful.
- This scales to very large workshops: seven groups of up to seven can get 50
  people to three agreed approaches in about two hours.
- **The six-and-90 rule** (James Macanufo): conversations don't get
  sufficiently deep with more than six people, and they run out of gas after
  about 90 minutes.
- For high-stakes meetings, hold **pre-meeting interviews** with attendees:
  keep an off-the-record tone, find a light personal connection, gather their
  expectations for the meeting, and ask clear direct questions. Use what you
  learn to build the agenda; never argue with interviewees or fix gaps during
  the interview — gaps are the raw material for the meeting itself.

### Time

- Default calendar blocks (30/60 min) are software defaults, not design
  decisions. Derive duration from the math instead: `content sections × 20
  minutes + transitions + share-outs`.
- Add 5–10 minutes per subgroup for share-outs, plus ~15 minutes for
  transitions and creative overflow in longer sessions.
- Breaks every 90 minutes in anything workshop-length.

## Meeting cost

Cost = attendees × duration × hourly rate — everyone's combined paycheck for
the time. Four plumbers meeting for an hour before a $50 faucet job triples the
job's cost. Also count the human cost: meetings fragment the ~6 hours of weekly
focused time people need, and each transition breaks flow. State the cost when
proposing (or challenging) a meeting; it disciplines invite lists and duration.

## Worked examples

**Fitting content to an hour (7 or fewer people):**
15 concepts ÷ 5 = 3 content sections × 20 min = 60 minutes. If you have 30
concepts, you need two hours, half the concepts, or parallel subgroups.

**Right-sizing a big meeting (15 people):**
Three groups of five, each covering the intended concepts at the standard
cadence (~60 min for 15 concepts), plus 5–10 minutes per group to share out
(~30 min) ≈ 90 minutes total.

**A presentation that kept derailing (the "Dave" case):**
8 people × 6 principles with detailed examples = 168 concept-agreements ≈ 11
hours at a sane pace — no wonder one hour failed. The redesign: overview all
six principles (10 min), discuss (10 min), then split into two role-based
subgroups (design vs. business) that each apply one principle at a time
(10 min apply + 10 min discuss), reconvening to share (20 min). Same hour,
achievable scope, and stakeholder disagreements isolated into the right room.

**A three-hour meeting that produced muddled action items (the "Jane" case):**
Redesign as 90 minutes with three 30-minute sections (one per team: 10 min
per list × 2 lists + 10 min reflection), with a public recorder building the
two lists on the wall in real time and a photo distributed immediately after —
turning fuzzy verbal instructions into an unambiguous shared artifact.
