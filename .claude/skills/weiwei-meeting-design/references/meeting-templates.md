# Meeting Templates: Beginning, Middle, and End

Source: *Meeting Design*, Chapters 6–9 (Kevin M. Hoffman). Each template gives
the meeting's goal, how to measure its outcome, and a sample agenda to adapt.
These are an à la carte menu — pick by stage of work and "sight distance"
(how far ahead the conversation looks), then tailor to the culture and outcome.

General rules for all beginning meetings: reveal and explore assumptions
first, then build agreement, then surface conflicts, then pursue decisions;
use advance work (interviews) to design the main event; prefer silent voting
methods to level power dynamics; and don't assume the person best at a task
is the right person to do it in the room (non-designers sketching, with
guidance, changes the conversation productively).

---

## Sales meeting (60 min)

**Goal:** Assess fit between two parties — like a date, not a pitch. Jumping
to budget/schedule prematurely fails; a good meeting leaves both sides wanting
to continue.
**Measure:** The relationship deepens relative to before — from a returned
email up to a requested statement of work. Decide in advance what the next
logical step would look like.

**Agenda:**
- **Introductions (10)** — each attendee: name, role, and one question they
  have about the effort. Capture visually.
- **Positioning statement (15)** — ≤5 key points on the proposed solution,
  focused on differentiators; simple statements, details on request.
- **Positioning reflection (15)** — revisit each point, soliciting feedback
  with designed questions: "This is based on assumptions about your business —
  are they correct?" "What does the best possible outcome look like?" "What
  happens after the engagement ends?"
- **Related work review (10)** — previous work, each item explicitly tied to
  the client's situation.
- **Final review of opening questions (10)** — answer ~5–6 of the collected
  questions; commit to answering the rest by email; name follow-ups for both
  sides.

Posture (Giles Colborne): listen first; poke each pain point to confirm the
client agrees it's worth solving ("What are the consequences of that?"); then
ask permission to help. If you're not the right fit, say so and refer —
honesty builds the longer relationship.

## Stakeholder interview (30–60 min)

**Goal:** Assess alignment between your understanding of the work and each
decision-maker's — usually the first meetings of a project, feeding the
kickoff agenda.
**Measure:** Number of expectation gaps identified. More gaps found = better
interview. Don't resolve gaps in the interview — collect them; the kickoff is
where they get addressed. Don't argue; you're there to listen.

**Agenda:** Introduction (≤5 min: purpose, "my goal is to listen," recording
permission per local law), then questions (25–55 min), e.g.:
- Tell me about your role / your vision for the work here.
- What's the most important way this project will change the organization?
- Who are the critical audiences? If you had to pick one primary?
- What information does this project need to succeed? What do you need that
  you don't have yet?
- What are the most important things people can do because of this project?
- What business cycles could change audience behavior?
- What puts this project at risk? What's the one thing we must get right?
- If risks are mitigated, what would make this wildly successful?

Keep an off-the-record, friendly tone; open with a light personal connection.

## Quickoff — quick kickoff (60 min)

**Goal:** Confirm shared assumptions and identify project risks — nothing
more. Discovering the group is *not* aligned counts as success (you found your
first risk). Requires real prep: full project brief distributed ≥2 days ahead;
no room for tangents.
**Measure:** Count of action items with deadlines, and their distribution —
few action items means poor alignment work; anyone leaving empty-handed
didn't need to attend.

**Agenda** (assumes ~3 phases/sprints):
- **Process overview (10)** — beginning and end of each major step; for
  deliverables: when final documents arrive and who approves; for agile: each
  sprint's goal and who declares it achieved.
- Per phase/sprint, repeat:
  - **Focus (7–8)** — key decisions and decision-makers; each person writes
    their action items + initials on their own color of sticky note, posted
    in sequence on the wall.
  - **Review (7–8)** — for each item: How will this be achieved? Does it
    depend on something else? Draw dependency lines; capture new questions in
    a fresh color.
- **Document** — photograph the final wall (it *is* the record); everyone
  takes/photographs their own notes; update the project tool (kanban, etc.).

## Brainstorm — KJ method (10–60 min)

**Goal:** Leave with more *viable* options than you started with — like a
campsite left better than found. Not the one perfect idea, and never a list
nobody evaluated. Spend as much effort agreeing on the problem as generating
answers.
**Measure:** Quantity of ideas × a simple viability check on each. One
facilitator per ~6 attendees scales this to large groups.

**Agenda:**
- **Introduction (≤15)** — one focusing question, discussed until everyone
  shares the same constraints. De-bias it: not "Why can't X work?" but "What
  could work for solving X?"
- **Generation (5–10)** — silent: one idea per sticky, big and legible, low
  detail (show an example of the right grain); then post all notes together.
- **Evaluation (10)** — group similar notes spatially; label groups if >5 or
  ambiguous; then **dot vote**: 3–5 dots each, stack-voting allowed —
  priorities expressed without talking, which levels power dynamics (for even
  flatter dynamics, keep the whole voting phase silent).
- **Discussion & closure (10–15, optional)** — review top vote-getters, pros
  and cons around the table; focus on eliminating weak options rather than
  arguing any single winner.

## OKR / strategy session (2 hours)

**Goal:** Build agreement on *objectives* — qualitative, ambitious, slightly
uncomfortable statements of why — before anyone touches key results or
tactics. Premature key results go to the parking lot.
**Measure:** Clarity of the resulting objectives: aligned with mission, still
within the realm of possibility, and endorsed by dot vote.

**Agenda:**
- **Frame the problem (20)** — circulate in advance: current key results (and
  why unacceptable), resources, principal (permanent) constraints, existing
  strategy — but no candidate new strategies. 5 min silent review; each
  person asks ≤3 questions; the group (not the facilitator) answers;
  questions stay visible throughout.
- **Capture all constraints (30)** — silent generation, one constraint per
  sticky, ≤5 each; post one person at a time with a verbal gloss; skip
  duplicates, rewrite variants; optionally group and label.
- **Remove constraints (30)** — rate each group's effect **minimal / notable
  / significant** with colored dots. Discuss how to remove or limit each; when
  tactics appear, climb the "why" ladder ("Why would that limit costs?" …)
  until a subjective value statement emerges — that's objective raw material.
  When people's "whys" differ, document the fork visibly.
- **Build objectives (40)** — subgroups take 1 significant + 1–2 lesser
  constraints, draft objective statements (20), then share out, post, and dot
  vote (20). Leave with the objectives the group finds most promising.

## Project kickoff workshop (4–8 hours)

**Goal:** Capture, categorize, explore, and expand every party's expectations;
produce aligned priorities and first design hypotheses. Costlier than a
quickoff and worth it: hours here save revisions later.
**Measure:** (a) A populated priority × feasibility matrix — including the
disagreements; (b) new ideas at sufficient fidelity for leadership approval.

**Prep — ARCI:** identify who is **A**ccountable (can sign off/declare
success), **R**esponsible (does the work), **C**onsulted (domain expertise),
**I**nformed (affected but not contributing). Interview A's and R's first,
then C's and I's (use the stakeholder-interview template). Mine transcripts
for expectations; categorize them; have future attendees score each 1–5 for
priority and 1–5 for feasibility via a form. Compute averages and standard
deviations — **high standard deviation (≈1.5+) = hidden disagreement**, the
most valuable thing to surface.

**Agenda:**
- **Introduction (30)** — "What would you like to accomplish in this
  workshop?" — each person aloud, captured with names, revisited at the end.
- **Priority/feasibility scoring (15, only if not done in advance)** + break
  to tally.
- **Score review (30)** — skim the low/low corner; dwell on middle and high
  bands and every high-σ item: why is this vital to one party and trivial to
  another? Identify conflicts; only resolve the trivially resolvable now.
- **Hypothesis design (1–2 h)** — Lean-UX style worksheet: audience, problem,
  qualitative + quantitative success measures. Solo first, then pairs (mixing
  disciplines/ARCI roles — never habitual collaborators), then fours merging
  into stronger hypotheses; converge to two, and have each group take
  ownership of the *other* group's hypothesis.
- **Break (15–30)** — every ~90 minutes, always.
- **Design studio (90 min–3 h)** — sketch against the hypotheses, alternating
  solo/group sketch and critique rounds with tight timeboxes (e.g., solo
  sketch 10 → pairs critique 10 → pairs sketch 15 → fours critique 15 → fours
  sketch 15 → eights critique 20 → eights sketch 20 → share 20). Never let a
  group exceed eight. Disarm "I'm not an artist": the output is conversation,
  not design; warm up with basic shapes. Variations: only designers sketch
  (transcribing the group's ideas), only non-designers sketch, or permanent
  marker only (no perfectionism).
- **Share-out with golden tickets (20–30)** — critique final sketches via
  stickies: "best part of this sketch" (color 1), "hardest part to do"
  (color 2), initials on every note; A-level stakeholders get 2–3
  visually-distinct "golden ticket" notes for executive must-haves, posted
  directly on the sketches. Photograph everything.
- **Break, then next steps (15)** — sequence from today's outputs to expected
  outcomes with owners. Then celebrate.

## Daily scrum (~10 min, ≤8 people)

**Goal:** Restart play from where work stopped — broadcast what was completed
yesterday, what's underway today, and what's blocked. No analysis; follow-up
conversations happen immediately *after*, protecting the team's time.
Observers (leadership) may watch, never contribute.
**Measure:** A kanban board (to do / doing / done): new items appearing in
column 1 and steady left-to-right flow = healthy; items stuck in "doing" =
enforce WIP limits (e.g., ≤3 items per person).

**Agenda:** Scrum master starts on time regardless of attendance. Round-robin
per person: yesterday's completions (cards moved), today's intentions (cards
into "doing," WIP limits checked), blockers (scrum master notes who needs to
talk to whom, after the scrum). Scrum master manages tasks, never people.

## Weekly project check-in (≤30 min)

**Goal:** Broadcast task status, upcoming milestones, and delays; manage
short-term expectations. Tune length/detail to culture — micromanaging
cultures want more, hands-off cultures less; if people call it a waste,
redesign it. Rotate the facilitator weekly so no one person owns "the truth."
Senior stakeholders present = more derailment; parking-lot their tangents into
a separate occasional stakeholder update.
**Measure:** Collect questions at the start; count how many the meeting
resolves.

**Agenda:**
- **Collect questions (5–10)** — everyone's, gathered visibly, unanswered.
- **Address questions (5–10)** — simple first, then harder; overly detailed
  dependencies to the parking lot.
- **Upcoming milestones (5–10)** — next efforts with owners; deliverables in
  the next two weeks; approval/feedback cycles that could block. Keep the list
  for next week's opening ("Where are we on this?").

Required attendance for the core team only; absentees get a summary of
actions taken + new action items with owners — never a transcript.

## Lean coffee (30–60 min)

**Goal:** Let the group's priorities drive the agenda and convert them into
owned tasks. Suits open, inconsistent attendance and long-running
coordination without a standing team.
**Measure:** Per meeting: topics raised (quantity) and vote concentration
(quality). Across meetings: topics retired. The same topics recurring forever
means the meeting isn't doing its job.

**Agenda** (reuse the same board every time — it's the group memory):
- **Capture topics (≤10)** — silent, one topic per card, written to be
  understandable out of context; resurrect prior topics as new cards.
- **Vote (≤5)** — 3 dots each (5 for groups under 10), stacking allowed.
- **Discuss (15–45)** — in vote order; zero-vote topics are dropped or
  shelved guilt-free (even their author preferred other things). Facilitate
  lightly; very large groups break out by topic (voters for topic A form
  group A).

## Deliverable presentation (30–60 min)

**Goal:** Feedback and/or approval on work embodied in an artifact (report,
deck, design, strategy). **Never read the deliverable aloud** — distribute it
at least two full working days ahead; the meeting is where the audience
*processes implications*, not where they meet the work. If approval doesn't
come, either the work wasn't ready or the presentation didn't convince.
**Measure:** Distance moved toward final approval, from where you stood
before the meeting.

**Agenda:**
- **Capture questions (5–10)** — collect everyone's questions aloud first,
  visibly, with names; equal count per person; no answers yet.
- **Short path (15–20):** re-sequence your material to answer the collected
  questions in logical order (simple → complex), checking them off visibly.
- **Long path — stories (20–40):** tell 2–3 five-minute stories from the work
  (context → conflict → resolution): the tough choices, surprises, and
  best-solved problems. Then **revisit questions (10–20)**: answer, check
  sufficiency with each asker, and capture new questions. Leaving with *more*
  questions is progress toward approval, not failure.

Bonus norm (Dana Chisnell): nobody attends a research-findings presentation
who hasn't observed at least one live research session.

## Design critique (2–4 hours)

**Goal:** A prioritized list of *actionable problems* — not solutions, which
short-circuit understanding; and not brutality, which damages the long
relationship between maker and approver.
**Measure:** Count of actionable problems, prioritized.

**Agenda** (screen-based work; facilitator needs design expertise):
- **Walkthrough (15–30)** — user goals and context, then every screen of the
  journey, all visible at once (large prints or fast-switchable projection).
- **Per-screen critique (10–20 each)** — three sticky colors, silent posting
  onto the screens themselves: what *cannot change* (1), what *could improve*
  (2), what *could be eliminated* (3). Facilitator consolidates duplicates and
  probes each with "why" (repeatedly if needed) to convert reactions into
  problems; collect problems on a separate visible list.
- **Prioritize (15–30)** — most/least serious first, then push for agreement
  on the middle; dot vote if needed; sequence the fixing effort by dots.

**Planning tool — work backward (Adam Connor):** pinpoint the actions the team
must take *immediately after* the meeting (confirm with them — mismatched
objectives sink workshops); ask what information/decisions those actions
require; repeat until the chain reaches what you already have. The gaps in
the chain select the activities. Use this whenever the user asks "what
meeting(s) do I need?" rather than "design this meeting."

## Build-your-own workshop (2 hours–2 days)

**Goal:** Manage long-term risk and create ideas impossible without mixed
perspectives (lateral thinking — diverse attendees disrupt each other's
mental loops just enough to spark the new); make decisions, simple or
complex.
**Measure:** Decisions made — and whether the outcome can be credited to any
one person (it shouldn't be). Watch the balance of saying/making/listening
per person; timebox contributions to force past the obvious.

**Design template:** Identify a framing question in advance; list the
decisions to make; pick attendees for varied perspectives via ARCI (a room of
similar minds needs no workshop). Never open with a presentation — ask people
what they want from the session and let them talk to each other first
(participation is expected and matters). Then sequence activities through
five spaces:
1. **Tone (30–60)** — how do they feel about the problem? Is it even the
   right problem?
2. **Motivation (30 min–2 h)** — who is served by solving it, who pays a
   cost, what drives them?
3. **Finding answers (1–3+ h)** — all possible solutions; how it's solved
   today and why.
4. **Implications (30–60)** — is this a symptom of deeper causes? Loop back
   to answers if needed, but protect time for step 5.
5. **Decisions (1–2 h)** — vote/gather input (dot voting; or "save one,
   eliminate one"); document decisions so they make sense out of context.

## UAT defect log review (20 min–1 hour)

**Goal:** Align on what "works well" means, assign every remaining defect an
owner, and capture rationale for deferrals (a parking lot that becomes the
future roadmap). Restating testing intentions up front prevents scope-creep
tangents — "wrong" means different things to developers and non-developers.
**Measure:** Two lists: open defects (should shrink toward zero between
meetings — if not, accountability isn't landing) and deferred items (small,
well-documented).

**Agenda** (run around a large visible log — grid of defect × status/severity
/dates; photograph at start and end of every meeting):
- **Review testing intentions (5)** — what counts as a bug here and what
  doesn't; capture borderline candidates.
- **Update statuses (20–50)** — rotate reporters: analyst 1 reports one
  defect → developers/analysts discuss → assign or defer (deferrals discussed
  against previous deferrals) → analyst 2 reports one… cycling until all are
  covered. Rotation keeps focus wide and voices varied.
- **Final decisions (5)** — revisit each deferral, assign owners most-severe
  first, photograph and distribute the updated board.

## Agile retrospective (40–60 min)

**Goal:** Team growth at sprint cadence: sort learnings into start / continue
/ stop; struggling teams use retros to find better ways of working, healthy
teams to document successes worth repeating.
**Measure:** Tie each change to a metric and score it next retro:
achieved ÷ target (e.g., aimed to cut sign-in failures 50%, cut 40% → 80%
effective). Effective retros produce sprints that meet targets.

**Agenda:**
- **Review prior goals (10, skip if first retro)** — score each previous
  change: under/met/exceeded.
- **Individual problems & changes (10–20)** — silent: problems from the
  sprint, a personal process change per problem, a measure per change; then
  share one change at a time so people can revise their own as they listen.
- **Team/system changes (10–20)** — write desired team-level changes on
  stickies; share with rationale; brainstorm metrics per change and settle on
  one KPI each (dot vote conflicts).
- **Celebrate & calibrate (10)** — revisit met/exceeded goals: good idea, or
  goal too easy? Toughen if needed; spotlight the work behind the wins.

## Postmortem (1–2 hours)

**Goal:** Uncover anything about the past that informs the future — so spend
*more time on the future than the past*. Play-by-play from memory breeds
blame; "how can we evolve?" doesn't. Postmortems are easy to skip (nothing
depends on them), and skipping them is how mistakes repeat.
**Measure:** Actionable insights per person — anyone with zero "things I'd do
differently" gets gently probed further; the facilitator seeks improvements
from *everyone*, not just people near the problems.

**Agenda** (whiteboard: names along the bottom, a team/department column at
the end, a horizon line halfway up):
- **List the best things (10–15)** — each person's favorite aspect of the
  project, above the horizon line; no "why" yet.
- **Discuss the best things (15–30)** — why each worked, from multiple
  perspectives. When one person's positive is another's negative (early
  finish = saved costs to the PM, felt rushed to the developer), that paradox
  is gold: post it below the line under the second person's name.
- **What could have been better (15–30)** — blameless framing: describe the
  *process*, not the person, that led to each problem; post below the line.
- **Actionable insights (20–45)** — walk each column; each person states what
  they'd do differently about their below-the-line items. Then throw a party.

**Blameless postmortem recipe (Etsy):** all parties give a detailed account of
(1) actions taken and when, (2) effects observed, (3) expectations held,
(4) assumptions made, (5) their understanding of the timeline — explicitly
without punishment or retribution. Circumstances, not people, cause failures;
rewarding an accurate story over finding a culprit gets you the details that
prevent recurrence ("bad apple" removal doesn't).

## Culture notes

Meetings are usability tests for an organization — the live evidence of the
gap between its espoused culture and how things actually get done. Patterns
worth borrowing when the real problem is cultural:

- **New to an organization?** Observe more, speak less in the first several
  routine meetings; note what behaviors get positive responses from leaders;
  adopt the local vocabulary rather than teaching yours (Aaron Irizarry, Dana
  Chisnell).
- **Building a new team's culture:** model desired behaviors in meetings
  rather than enforcing them; rotate facilitation to anyone who wants
  practice — it's a marketable skill and distributes ownership (Leslie
  Jensen-Inman).
- **Knowledge silos:** an open cross-department meeting around shared data or
  practice (MailChimp's "data nerd lunch") makes sharing teams *more*
  influential, reversing the hoarding incentive.
- **Executive whims derail everything:** an outsider modeling "no, not right
  now" — halting the meeting, capturing the disagreement, restating the
  outcome — can permanently change what a team believes it's allowed to do.
  A related fix: never commit to deadlines inside meetings; commit to tasks,
  then follow up with dates after assessing the larger effort (Aaron
  Parkening's client).
- **Orienting people to change** (Karen McGrane): don't make people feel
  dumb — level-set first; data alone doesn't persuade; you can't argue
  through fear — address the unspoken risk to reputations; motivate by
  articulating a pain the team already feels and fixing it.
- **Confronting an ugly problem:** a workshop that maps the whole process
  with everyone whose work intertwines (many meeting for the first time) can
  re-energize a stuck bureaucracy — visualizing the flow together surfaces
  redundancies no silo could see (Jesse Taggert's 700→150 backlog case).
