## Conflict is material, not failure

Without addressing, exploring, and resolving conflict, work doesn't get done —
ignored conflicts resurface in the work repeatedly. Some conflict is structural
(client wants maximum value for minimum spend; consultant wants room to do the
work well); well-run meetings define and depersonalize it. Beware also that
someone who *anticipates* conflict is the most likely person to initiate it.
Facilitation is the tool that lets a group make good decisions while giving
real conflicts the respect they deserve.

## The four roles (Doyle & Strauss)

1. **Facilitator** — explicitly designated, runs the process: presents the
   structure, guides the group through it, manages who speaks and for how
   long, offers non-speaking ways to contribute, and stays **neutral**.
   Explicitly naming a facilitator at the start of every meeting is a small
   change with outsized returns.
2. **Recorder** — creates the public, visible, real-time record of main ideas,
   conflicts, and decisions ("group memory"). Not a transcriptionist: judgment
   about what's worth writing is the job. Group members should correct the
   record when it misses their point.
3. **Leader** — the stakeholder accountable for the outcome. States the intent
   (ideally before the meeting), then either stays away — attendees take more
   risks when they don't feel evaluated — or attends *as a group member*,
   holding major pronouncements until the conversation has run its pattern.
4. **Group members** — contribute, request corrections to the record, and
   watch the facilitation itself for bias.

Small meetings can combine facilitator + recorder; large ones need one
facilitator per subgroup (roughly one per six attendees).

## Common facilitation mistakes

- **Facilitating your own pitch.** If you're invested in the outcome, the
  group detects bias and stops trusting the process. Hand facilitation to a
  neutral party and take a subject-matter-expert or advocate seat instead —
  or, at minimum, bring an SME so you can focus on the conversation.
- **Deciding the structure alone.** Collaborate with the leader/stakeholders
  on what outcome the meeting must produce; they define the outcome, the
  facilitator designs the experience that gets there.
- **Private notes as the record.** A scribe's laptop notes visible to one
  person create no group memory and rarely get reread.
- **Leaders looming.** Stakeholders attending "to check in" shift the meeting
  from its outcome to pleasing them. Recast them as group members or excuse
  them.

## Divergence and convergence

The productive conversation pattern (after Kaner's *Facilitator's Guide to
Participatory Decision-Making*): first **diverge** — increase the quantity and
diversity of ideas — then **converge** — increase quality by prioritizing and
pruning.

- During divergence: suspend judgment, welcome tangents (they're how novel
  ideas arrive), tolerate unresolved contradiction, push past the obvious.
- **The turn**: the facilitator tracks roughly how many ideas are in play and
  their quality, then declares the corner — time to collect tangents and turn
  them into a direction or decision.
- During convergence: summarize each option's essence, eliminate what's least
  likely to succeed, decide what's kept and how to act on it.
- **Sequence agendas divergence-first.** Divergence-only = brainstorming
  clichés with no accountability. Convergence-only = "eliminating options"
  from the start; people tune out because there's no room to add anything.
- **Parking lot**: capture valuable-but-tangential ideas visibly, and never
  end without a plan for each parked item — otherwise the lot is a trash can
  with better branding.

## Question design (humble inquiry)

Most questions asked in meetings are "proud inquiry" — the asker already has an
answer in mind and is steering. Attendees notice, and stop believing their
input matters. Design questions instead (after Edgar Schein's humble inquiry),
in four classes:

- **Feelings** — "When we discovered the Q1 loss, how did everyone feel?" /
  "When a customer first opens the app, how should they feel?" Use when strong
  emotions are in the room and tackling them directly would be productive.
- **Motivations** — "What were you hoping would happen when the budget was
  cut?" / "What were you going for with this direction?" Use to judge whether
  a tangent merits pursuing and to surface unshared assumptions.
- **Actions** — "What's the first step in executing this?" / "What does our
  audience do to solve this today, without us?" Use to reach the right level
  of detail — more detail for concrete processes, less for fuzzy futures.
- **Systems** — "If we nail these six user stories, how do we prioritize
  functionality?" / "What leadership concerns could impact this direction?"
  Use to explore interdependencies and to align people with very different
  perspectives ("OK, then what's next? How does one thing affect the other?").

Method: start from the meeting's intended outcomes, draft the questions you'd
naturally ask (biases and all), then strip the embedded assumptions, then
reword each toward one of the four classes. No need to cover all four in one
meeting.

## The three style spectrums

Facilitation style is a set of knobs, not a fixed identity. Assess where you
(or the chosen facilitator) sit, and adjust toward what the meeting needs.

- **Scripted ↔ Improvisational.** Scripts bring welcome clarity to meandering
  meeting cultures but can read as inflexible or inauthentic. Improvisers read
  the room and adapt, but need rapid feedback and can lose opaque or
  agenda-clinging groups. Middle path: a **branching agenda** — a diagram of
  3–4 steps with hypothetical paths, choose-your-own-adventure style. If you
  improvise, sketch a backup script (list what could go wrong; prepare a
  question or two per scenario). Deciding to abandon a script is easy;
  conjuring one you never wrote is not.
- **Drawing ↔ Speaking.** Visual facilitation exploits the brain's spatial
  strengths: it forces synthesis (you can't draw everything), creates a map of
  the discussion, and hand-drawn marks invite participation in a way polished
  artifacts don't. Constraints: varying visual ability and accessibility, and
  cultures that read drawing as "touchy-feely." Speakers weave meaning from
  tone and body language but exhaust the room and miss quiet reflection.
  Blend: speakers should sketch simple flows; drawers should title each
  "chapter" of sketches aloud and pose questions at start, midpoint, and end.
- **Space-making ↔ Space-filling.** Space makers allow pauses and reflection —
  good for developing options and thorough lists; they lose control to
  think-aloud personalities. Space fillers keep density and momentum — good
  for prioritization and decisions; they fail when the group lacks shared
  concepts and steamroll the silence where better answers were forming. When
  stuck: fillers should force themselves to stop talking; makers should switch
  gears when one voice starts dictating the rhythm.

Also worth stealing from GE's facilitation-competency practice: gather
background material before the meeting, curate activities per audience
(strategic activities for executives, operational for operators), run
"meetings before the meeting," and choreograph carefully enough that it
doesn't feel choreographed (e.g., different-colored pens per person so you
know who wrote what; engineered subgroup composition mixing skills, influence,
and chemistry).

## Remote meetings

- **Recording**: use shared documents/whiteboards broadcast to every screen.
  The recorder can't assume attention — periodically check in ("Are these the
  ideas so far? Does this reflect the decisions?") and reserve a few minutes
  at the end for a point-by-point review of what was captured.
- **Facilitating**: trust normally built through body language must come from
  explicit ground rules — e.g., say your name before speaking until voices
  are known. Tools that show who's speaking (and percentage of talk time per
  person) help both fairness and facilitator self-awareness.
- **Shared sketching**: if digital whiteboards fail, thick black marker on
  white paper or 3×5 cards held to the camera stays legible on any
  connection.

## Working with discomfort

(After Sarah B. Nelson.) Novel ideas trip a hardwired alarm — the "creativity
bias": people reject the new subconsciously and then construct logical-sounding
arguments against it. Discomfort and creativity travel together; a perfectly
comfortable team probably isn't pushing hard enough.

- **Prime it**: "You'll likely feel pushed in this process, and it will be
  uncomfortable. That's normal in creative work. When it happens, I'll guide
  you through it — do I have your permission?" This builds trust before it's
  needed.
- **Name it in the moment**: when the room goes silent, people shift in
  seats, or combat breaks out — interrupt: "How's everyone feeling? A little
  uncomfortable? Perfect, we're in the right place. What do we need to do to
  get past this?" If that's not enough, take a break.
- **Seed it deliberately**: identify the elephants in the room in advance
  (pre-interviews reveal them) and find a concrete way to put one in the room
  — e.g., an empty chair with the absent executive's name on a sticky note and
  the question "How does this topic impact the process you're designing?"
  Sometimes planting the seed is the whole job.

## Intense conflict: nonviolent communication

(After Ben Sauer / Marshall Rosenberg.) For end-stage or high-stakes conflict
where identities and jobs feel on the line. The goal is understanding, never
persuasion, and it works even if only the facilitator uses it.

Expressing yourself: observations without judgments ("When I see the deadline
slip" vs. "When you blow the deadline"); feelings without attribution ("I'm
frustrated" vs. "You're doing this deliberately"); universal needs ("I need
some predictability"); a clear positive request, not a negative demand
("Could you flag risks on Mondays?" vs. "Stop surprising me").

Hearing others: guess at the feeling and need, neutrally, and check — "You
seem really frustrated. What is it you're wanting from us?" Visible relief
usually follows a genuine connection; then the practical conversation can
restart.

Related: anger in meetings usually comes from someone doing what they believe
is best for the group's success or survival. Meet them at the reasoning that
led there rather than debating the conclusion — it keeps your own head cool.
